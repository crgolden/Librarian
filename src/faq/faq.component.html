Frequently Asked Questions

Linking your PlayStation account means giving Librarian a token called an NPSSO. That's a reasonable thing to be cautious about, so here's exactly what happens when you do it.

Who is Librarian for?

Anyone with a PSN account that's been around for a while. Between everything you've bought over the years and any free-to-play games you've downloaded, that's usually more than people expect on its own. If you subscribe to PlayStation Plus it grows further: the Monthly Games you claim — a few every month — are yours to keep and play for as long as you stay subscribed, and on Extra or Premium you can add hundreds more Game Catalog titles as entitlements (Premium also gets Game Trials, so you can sample a new or upcoming title before deciding whether to add it to your collection). It gets harder to keep track of once you're splitting a library across more than one console or a drive of extended storage. Librarian pulls all of it into one place so you can see what you have and decide what to play next. You don't need PlayStation Plus to use it — it works the same either way, there's just more to work with if you have it.

What is an NPSSO token?

It's a session token Sony issues after you sign in at playstation.com. It works like a password — anyone who has it can act as you on PlayStation Network until it expires (about 60 days). Treat it the same way you'd treat a password: don't paste it into a site you don't trust, and don't share it.

Why do you need this, when some other PlayStation trophy sites don't ask for anything?

Sites like that use one shared PlayStation account to look things up for everyone. That means they never see anything of yours — but it also means everyone is sharing that one account's speed limit, which is why those sites make you wait for updates, or charge extra to skip the wait. Librarian works differently: because you give us your own token, Curator can check PlayStation using your account instead of a shared one, so your library updates as fast as your own account allows, with nothing to wait for and nothing to pay to skip it. That speed comes from trusting us with something more powerful than those other sites ever ask for, and that's exactly why the rest of this page — and the actual code behind it, which you can go read yourself — exists.

How do I get mine?

Sign in at playstation.com, then in that same browser (not a different one, not incognito) visit https://ca.account.sony.com/api/v1/ssocookie. The page returns a short block of JSON with an npsso field — that value is your token. Paste it into the PSN link form on your PSN settings page.

What does Librarian do with it?

Librarian passes your NPSSO to Curator, the API that actually talks to PlayStation. Curator uses it once, immediately, to ask Sony for an access token and a refresh token — then discards the NPSSO. It is never written to a database and never appears in a log line anywhere in Curator's code.

Why does linking need my email at all?

Before Curator keeps anything, it checks that the email PlayStation has on file for your account — and confirms is verified — matches the email your sign-in already established. That comparison happens in memory and is never written down; there is no email column anywhere in Curator's database. If the emails don't match, or PSN reports the email as unverified, the tokens Curator just obtained are deleted immediately and nothing is linked.

What happens to the access and refresh tokens?

They're encrypted before they're stored, and only Curator holds the key. You can see when they expire on your PSN settings page.

How do I revoke access?

Two options, and you can use either or both. Unlink on your PSN settings page deletes Curator's copy of your tokens right away. Sony doesn't give third parties a way to revoke a token remotely, so if you want to be certain the NPSSO itself can't be used by anyone, sign out of all devices at playstation.com — that invalidates it at the source.

Can I see everything Curator has done with my account?

Yes. Every linking attempt, unlink, library refresh, and trophy fetch Curator performs on your behalf is recorded in an action log you can view and download yourself — see the privacy policy for what's in it and how long it's kept.

What are the RAWG/OpenCritic keys on my PSN settings page for?

They're entirely optional. Curator can enrich your library with genre and critic-score data from RAWG and OpenCritic, but it doesn't provision a shared key for every user — that wouldn't scale. If you want that enrichment, you can provide your own free RAWG and/or OpenCritic key. If you skip this, your library still works — you just won't get that extra metadata for titles nobody's key has resolved yet.

How does Curator know my key is valid?

When you save a key, Curator makes one small request to that provider to confirm it's accepted before storing anything. If the provider rejects it, you'll see that immediately and the key is never saved — so you always have a chance to correct it, instead of finding out only when a later library refresh fails.

Is my RAWG/OpenCritic key ever shown to me again, or logged anywhere?

No. It's encrypted the moment you save it, the same way your PlayStation tokens are. Your PSN settings page only ever shows whether a key is configured and when it was added — never the value, before or after saving.

What happens to the data my key retrieves?

Only the game metadata it retrieves (title, genre, critic score) — never your key. That metadata is added to Curator's shared game catalog so future lookups for that same game, by any user, don't need to call RAWG/OpenCritic again. This is why some of your games may already show enrichment data even if you've never added a key — someone else's key, or Curator's own periodic catalog refresh, already resolved it.

How do I remove a RAWG/OpenCritic key?

From your PSN settings page — each key has its own "Remove" button, independent of the other. Removing one doesn't affect the other or anything else in your account.

Why doesn't my whole library get enriched right away?

RAWG is fast, but OpenCritic's free tier is limited, so OpenCritic data for a very large or brand-new library fills in gradually across a few refreshes rather than all at once — contributed cooperatively, a bit at a time, by everyone's refreshes. Your library page shows a RAWG and OpenCritic rating per game, with a dash for whichever hasn't resolved yet, so you can always see exactly what's been resolved so far.

What do AAA, AA, and Indie mean next to a game?

That label is Curator's own, not PlayStation's and not a review site's, so here's exactly how it's decided. It comes from the game's publisher — or its developer, when we don't have a publisher — matched against a list of names. Match a name on the AAA list and the game is AAA; match the AA list and it's AA; match neither and it's Indie. AAA is checked first, so a name on both lists counts as AAA. If we don't know who published a game at all, it gets no tier rather than being guessed as Indie.

Two things worth being clear about. It describes the publisher, not the game — an excellent game from a small studio is still "Indie", and that isn't a comment on quality. And because it's a list of names we maintain, it can be wrong or out of date; when we change the list, games classified under the old one are reclassified to match.

Where does the "Minimum score" number in a collection come from?

It's an average of the ratings we have for a game: RAWG's critic score, OpenCritic's top-critic score, and the PlayStation Store's own star rating. Whichever of those exist are averaged, and the ones that don't are left out rather than counted as zero. The Store's rating uses a 1–5 scale, so it's converted to the same 0–100 scale as the other two first.

So a game with all three ratings has a more dependable number behind it than one resting on a single source, and a game with none has no score at all rather than a zero. The Store's own rating always counts toward it — nothing from RAWG or OpenCritic replaces what PlayStation already says about a game, those only fill in what PlayStation doesn't provide.

How do games get into the public catalog?

Two ways. Curator walks the PlayStation Store's own public category listings directly, so most of the catalog exists before anyone's library ever touches it — no PSN account is needed to browse it. The rest arrives naturally: when someone links their library, whatever games Curator finds there that aren't already in the catalog get added too. Either way, a title is matched by name (and, when it's available, PlayStation's own internal id for that game) so the same game arriving through both a Store walk and someone's library doesn't end up listed twice.

What actually makes it in still has to clear Curator's usual filtering for what counts as a real game. Games Curator finds by walking the Store directly are checked against PlayStation's own full-game classification, which excludes add-ons and DLC. Games that arrive because they turned up in someone's library go through the same free-to-play-title and media-app exclusions your own library does.

Does every catalog game have its own page?

Yes. Each game has a permanent address of its own, so you can link to a specific title rather than to a search you'd have to repeat. Those pages need no account — signed out you'll see the game's cover art, franchise, genre, tier, all three review scores, and a link to its PlayStation Store page when one is known.

Signed in, one extra line appears on a game you own: your own trophy progress for it. That's the only part of the page that differs between visitors, and it's only ever shown to you — see the privacy policy for exactly how that works. If you've never turned trophy harvesting on in PSN Settings, there's no progress to show and the line is simply absent.

Where do a catalog game's franchise, genre, and tier come from?

Franchise comes from checking the title against an ordered list of name patterns Curator maintains — the first pattern that matches wins, and a title that matches nothing just shows no franchise. It's text matching against the title alone, and when that list changes, every catalog game is rechecked against it rather than staying stuck with an older answer.

Genre works differently, because the PlayStation Store is the source of truth here: if the Store's own catalog has genre tags for a title, Curator uses those. Only when the Store doesn't have genre data for a title yet does Curator fall back to RAWG's community-tagged genres. Either source can list more than one tag for a game; Curator shows the most specific one (preferring, say, "Racing" over a generic "Simulation" tag when both apply).

The AAA/AA/Indie tier next to a game works the same way here as everywhere else in Librarian — see the question above for exactly how that's decided.

Why does a catalog listing show three separate scores?

RAWG, OpenCritic, and the PlayStation Store's own star rating, shown side by side exactly as each source reports them — a dash means Curator hasn't resolved that source for this title yet, not that the score is zero. That's different from the blended "Minimum score" number collections use to filter (see the question above): here nothing is averaged together, and none of the three ever overwrites another. The Store's rating in particular is never adjusted or replaced by anything from RAWG or OpenCritic — it's shown exactly as PlayStation itself reports it.

Can I keep PS5 games on an external drive?

Yes, and from the consoles & storage page Librarian tracks them there — you just can't play them from it. Parking games you've finished on a USB drive is what extended storage is for, and it saves re-downloading something large later, but a PS5 game has to be copied back to the internal SSD or an M.2 drive before it'll run. PS4 games have no such restriction; those play straight off USB.

So a PS5 game on your external drive shows up as installed there, taking up that drive's space — Librarian doesn't currently flag it as "needs moving" versus ready to play, so that part's on you to remember. M.2 drives are different from USB in this respect — PS5 games play directly from those, so anything on an M.2 is ready to go.

Drives that come out of the console keep what's on them. Move an M.2 or a USB drive to another PlayStation and your games come with it, so Librarian follows the drive rather than the console — attach it to a different console from the consoles & storage page and its tracked installs simply count toward whichever console it's plugged into now, with nothing to re-mark by hand.

What's on my public profile?

Your profile is private until you turn it on yourself, on your profile settings page. Once public, it can show your PSN account id (or "Unlinked user" if you haven't linked one), your library, your saved collections, your trophy summary, and your PSN online id — each one is its own toggle, and each one is off by default. Your follower and following lists are always visible to anyone, public profile or not, since that's Librarian's own data and has nothing to do with PlayStation. Your region is never shown anywhere in Librarian, on your own profile or anyone else's.

Collections have their own, second layer on top of that account-wide toggle: even with "show collections" on, only the collections you've individually set to public (from that collection's own page) actually appear on your profile. A private or unlisted collection stays off your profile regardless of your account setting — only its direct link, if you've shared one, opens it.

How does Librarian show someone else's trophies without their password?

It doesn't need their password, or their PSN session at all. When you view someone's public profile and they've chosen to show their trophies, Librarian looks them up using your own linked PlayStation account — the same way looking up any other player's public trophies works on PSN itself. Nothing of theirs is ever touched. If you haven't linked your own PSN account, or your link has expired, that section just doesn't show — you won't see an error, and nobody is notified.

How does follow/unfollow work?

You can follow anyone, whether or not their profile is public — following doesn't require the other person to show you anything. Follower and following lists are always visible on every profile, so you can always see who follows someone and who they follow. Following someone you already follow, or unfollowing someone you don't, is harmless — it just does nothing.

You can also follow an individual collection rather than a whole person — from a collection's own page (public or unlisted, never private) or its share link, even one you opened before signing in; signing in from there brings you right back to it. Unfollowing works even if the collection has since been made private again; "Collections I follow" on your own collections page always lists what you've followed, whether or not it's still reachable any other way.

Do you show ads, or track me for advertisers?

No, and we never will. There's no ad network on this site, no ad-tracking or retargeting cookies, and your account, your library, and your PSN data are never shared with an advertiser or sold to anyone. Some other trophy-tracking sites run on ad revenue, which is a reasonable way to pay the bills, but it's not how this one works or will ever work.

Is this actually safe, or am I just supposed to trust you?

Don't take our word for it — both apps are open source. Librarian (this site, including the BFF that proxies your requests) is at github.com/crgolden/Librarian, and Curator (the API that handles your NPSSO, verifies your email, and stores your tokens) is at github.com/crgolden/Curator. Every claim on this page and in the privacy policy is something you can go read in the code.
